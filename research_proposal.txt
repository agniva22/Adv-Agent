================================================================================
SAFE LEARNED AGGREGATION FOR RESILIENT DISTRIBUTED OPTIMIZATION
================================================================================


1. SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We will build a learned, adversarially trained aggregation rule for resilient
distributed optimization. By construction, it keeps the safety guarantees of
Local Filtering (LF) [1]. We will measure how close it gets to the best
accuracy any secure algorithm can reach.

The plan has three parts:

  1. A reinforcement-learning (RL) attacker that finds the strongest attacks
     against LF-based optimization.
  2. An RL defender whose weights are confined to the LF-feasible set, so the
     consensus and safety theorems of [1] hold for every policy it can learn.
  3. A zero-sum game between the two, trained end to end and evaluated
     against the |T|/n optimality lower bound of [1, Prop. 8.1].

To our knowledge, no prior work combines these. The closest learning-based
defences [7], [10] address pure consensus. They either give no guarantees [7]
or are evaluated only against fixed-signal attacks [10].


2. BACKGROUND
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The setting is that of Sundaram and Gharesifard [1]. It is scalar,
unconstrained distributed optimization over a fixed directed graph G = (V, E),
in which some nodes are adversarial.

- Goal. Each regular node v_i holds a private convex function f_i with
  subgradients bounded by L. Together the regular nodes seek a minimizer of
  the average of their functions.

- Adversaries. They may know everything and coordinate. Malicious nodes
  broadcast one value to all neighbours; Byzantine nodes may send different
  values to different neighbours. The adversary set is either F-total (at most
  F nodes overall) or F-local (at most F in any regular node's
  in-neighbourhood).

- Standard algorithms break. Distributed subgradient descent [3], [4] is
  captured by a single node that holds its value fixed [1, Prop. 4.3]. Worse,
  any algorithm that is exactly optimal without adversaries can be steered
  anywhere by one undetectable adversary [1, Thm. 4.4].

- LF dynamics. Each regular node discards up to F neighbour values above its
  own and up to F below. It then takes a convex combination of the rest, with
  weights at least eta, and a subgradient step:

      x_i(t+1) = a_ii(t) x_i(t) + sum_{j in J_i(t)} a_ij(t) x_j(t) - alpha_t d_i(t)

- Guarantees of LF.
    * Consensus for F-local Byzantine adversaries on (2F+1)-robust graphs
      [1, Thm. 6.1].
    * For F-total malicious adversaries, (F+1, F+1)-robustness is necessary
      and sufficient for consensus [1, Thm. 6.4].
    * With sum alpha_t = infinity and alpha_t -> 0, the regular nodes converge
      into the convex hull of their local minimizers [1, Thm. 7.1].

- What LF does not give.
    * No convergence to a single point: one malicious node can force a
      permanent oscillation [1, Ex. 7.2].
    * No optimality: any hull-respecting algorithm can be forced to an error
      of |T|/n * |b - a|, where T is a maximum F-local set [1, Prop. 8.1].
      Finding T is NP-hard [1, Thm. 8.4].

The fact this project relies on: the theorems of [1] hold for ANY weights that
form a convex combination over the retained set with every weight at least
eta. The weights are therefore a free design variable that can be learned
without losing the guarantees.


3. RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAP
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
No prior work found learns the aggregation rule for resilient distributed
OPTIMIZATION, keeps provable safety, and trains it against a learned
multi-step attacker. Learned defences exist only for pure consensus [7], [8],
[9], [10], and learned attackers only for other settings [7], [14].

Comparison of prior work
  (Problem | Defender | Attacker | Guarantees | Optimality measured)

  [1]  Sundaram & Gharesifard 2019
       Optimization | Fixed LF trimming | Worst-case, analytic |
       Consensus, convex hull | Lower bound only (Prop. 8.1)

  [2]  LeBlanc et al. 2013
       Consensus | Fixed W-MSR trimming | Worst-case, analytic |
       Consensus, safety | No

  [5], [6]  Su & Vaidya 2021; Zhao et al. 2020
       Optimization | Fixed filtering | Worst-case, analytic |
       Convergence to a region | Bounds

  [7]  Hou et al., AAAI 2021
       Consensus | RL weights (D-DDPG, Q-consensus) |
       Constant, random, RL faulty nodes | None | No

  [9]  Fung et al. 2022
       Consensus in MARL | RL trust selection | Unreliable agents |
       None | No

  [10] Zhang, Chen, Ji, IEEE L-CSS 2026
       Consensus | Online MILP weights, Stackelberg game |
       Myopic best response; fixed or sinusoidal signal in simulations |
       Consensus, O(sqrt T) regret (see G2) | No

  [11], [12], [13]  Resilient MARL
       Multi-agent RL | Filtering protects learning | Byzantine messages |
       Convergence | No

  [14] Figura et al. 2021
       Consensus-based MARL | None | Single adversarial learner |
       None | No

  THIS PROJECT
       Optimization | RL weights inside the LF shield |
       RL, multi-step, stealthy | Inherited from [1] |
       Against the |T|/n bound

The gaps:

  G1 - No learned defence for resilient optimization.
       [7] and [10] only handle consensus: no local costs and no gradient term.

  G2 - Learned defences lack certificates.
       [7] gives no guarantee, and counts convergence to a constant faulty
       node as success. [10] proves safety, but its Prop. 1 case (ii) uses the
       non-convex combination 2x_i - x_r, so the convex-hull argument appears
       not to hold. This needs checking.

  G3 - No strong learned attacker for LF/MSR-type optimization.
       Existing evaluations use fixed, random or sinusoidal signals. Nobody
       has searched for trajectory-level attacks such as the oscillation of
       [1, Ex. 7.2] or the stealthy fake-cost attack of [1, Thm. 4.4].

  G4 - Accuracy is never compared with what is achievable.
       The |T|/n bound of [1] has not been used as a yardstick for any learned
       or game-based method.


4. RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND CONTRIBUTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We ask four questions, one per gap.

  RQ1 (gap G3)
      How much can an RL attacker degrade LF optimization (bias, oscillation,
      slowdown) while staying inside the bounds of [1]?
      -> C1. An RL attack benchmark that recovers the attacks of
             [1, Ex. 7.2, Thm. 4.4] and finds stronger ones.

  RQ2 (gaps G2, G3)
      Do existing learned or game-based defences [7], [10] stay safe against
      such an attacker?
      -> C2. A stress test that exposes failures of uncertified defences, or
             confirms their claims.

  RQ3 (gaps G1, G2)
      Can a defender learned inside the LF shield cut the optimality error
      while provably keeping consensus and safety?
      -> C3. A shielded-defender theorem plus the learned aggregator itself.

  RQ4 (gap G4)
      Trained in a zero-sum game, how close does the defender get to the
      |T|/n lower bound?
      -> C4. A game formulation, a training method, and an empirical gap to
             the bound across graph families.

C3 is the theoretical core. It needs a short proof that every action in the
shielded set meets the hypotheses of [1, Thms. 6.1, 6.4, 7.1]. Everything else
is empirical.


5. PROBLEM FORMULATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The same simulated plant serves three learning problems. Each episode samples
a graph G, local functions {f_i}, and an adversary set A (F-local or F-total).
It then runs T steps with alpha_t = c/(t+1).

5A. Attacker MDP (RQ1, RQ2)

  The regular nodes run a fixed defence (LF, [7] or [10]). The adversaries act
  as one centralized learner, which matches the omniscient model of [1].

  - State: s_t = (x_R(t), x_A(t), t), plus the episode's graph and function
    parameters.
  - Action: for malicious nodes, one value per adversary (u_t in R^|A|); for
    Byzantine nodes, one value per outgoing edge of each adversary.
  - Transition: the defence's update rule, which is deterministic.
  - Reward, one of three:
      * bias: |xbar_R(T) - x*|, or f(xbar_R(T)) - f(x*);
      * non-convergence: sum_t |xbar_R(t+1) - xbar_R(t)|;
      * stealth-penalized: either of the above, minus a charge each time a
        regular node filters out the attacker's value.
  - Sanity check: against LF, no attacker can push states outside the hull
    of the regular minimizers [1, Thm. 7.1]. A violation means a simulator
    bug.

5B. Shielded defender (RQ3)

  Each regular node runs the same policy pi_theta (shared parameters),
  executed in a decentralized way.

  - Observation o_i(t): its own state x_i(t), its sorted in-neighbour values,
    its subgradient d_i(t), the step size alpha_t, and optionally a short
    history window.
  - Shield (shielding in the sense of safe RL [18]): apply the LF filter
    first to get the retained set J_i(t). The policy then outputs logits over
    {i} U J_i(t). These are mapped to weights by a floored softmax, so every
    weight is at least eta and the weights sum to 1:

      a_ij(t) = eta + (1 - (|J_i(t)| + 1) * eta) * softmax_j( pi_theta(o_i(t)) )

  - Claim to prove (C3): for every theta, the closed loop satisfies the
    hypotheses of [1, Thms. 6.1, 6.4, 7.1]. So consensus and convergence into
    the hull hold for any learned policy, even one being exploited by an
    adversary.
  - Reward (shared): -sum_i (x_i(T) - x*)^2 - lambda * sum_t D(t), where D(t)
    is the gap between the largest and smallest regular state.

5C. Zero-sum game (RQ4)

      min_{pi_D} max_{pi_A}  E[ f(xbar_R(T)) - f(x*) ]
      subject to: A is F-local (or F-total)

  - Training: alternating best responses, PSRO [20], or robust multi-agent
    actor-critic in the style of M3DDPG [22] or RARL [21]. Optionally the
    attacker also chooses where the adversaries sit.
  - Yardsticks: plain LF is the upper reference. The |T|/n * |b - a| bound of
    [1, Prop. 8.1] is the lower reference: no hull-respecting defender can
    beat it.


6. WORKFLOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The work runs in nine phases. Two gates keep us from building on unverified
ground: the simulator must reproduce [1] before any learning, and the shield
theorem must be proved before game training.

  +----------------------+     +----------------------+     +----------------------+
  | 0. Literature check  | --> | 1. Simulator         | --> | 2. Baselines         |
  | Confirm gaps G1-G4   |     | LF, graphs, f_i      |     | DGD, LF, Q-consensus,|
  | Citation sweep       |     | Reproduce Ex 6.2,    |     | DRC-RSSG, Su-Vaidya  |
  |                      |     | 7.2, 8.2             |     |                      |
  +----------------------+     +----------------------+     +----------------------+
                                                                       |
            GATE 1: simulator reproduces the results of [1]            |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
  v
  +----------------------+     +----------------------+     +======================+
  | 3. Scripted attacks  | --> | 4. RL attacker       | --> | 5. Shielded defender |
  | Fixed, random,       |     | Attack every         |     | Prove the shield     |
  | switching, stealthy  |     | baseline (RQ1, RQ2)  |     | theorem (CORE)       |
  +----------------------+     +----------------------+     +======================+
                                                                       |
            GATE 2: shield theorem proved and checked                  |
  +--------------------------------------------------------------------+
  v
  +----------------------+     +----------------------+     +----------------------+
  | 6. Zero-sum training | --> | 7. Evaluation        | --> | 8. Paper             |
  | Defender vs RL       |     | Gap to |T|/n bound,  |     | Write-up and code    |
  | attacker (BR / PSRO) |     | ablations            |     | release              |
  +----------------------+     +----------------------+     +----------------------+

Phase details:

  Phase 0 - Literature check
    - Run a Google Scholar sweep of papers citing [1] and [2], filtered by
      learning, RL or game terms.
    - Read [10] Prop. 1 in full, plus the full texts of [11] and [12].
    - Exit: no paper covers G1-G4 together; the related-work table is final.

  Phase 1 - Simulator
    - Build a vectorized Python/NumPy environment with a Gymnasium/PettingZoo
      interface covering LF dynamics, Byzantine and malicious adversaries, and
      capped quadratic f_i.
    - Add utilities: a robustness checker for small graphs, and a maximum
      F-local set solver (integer program) for computing |T|.
    - Exit: reproduce LF consensus on the Fig. 1 graph (Ex. 6.2), the
      oscillation of Ex. 7.2, the b/4 error of Ex. 8.2, and the capture
      result of Prop. 4.3.

  Phase 2 - Baselines
    - Implement DGD [3], LF [1], Q-consensus [7], DRC-RSSG [10] and
      Su-Vaidya [5].
    - Add the gradient step to [7] and [10], and state this adaptation
      explicitly in the paper.
    - Exit: each baseline reproduces its own paper's headline result in its
      original setting.

  Phase 3 - Scripted attack benchmark
    - Implement attacks: fixed value, random, switching (Ex. 7.2), stealthy
      fake cost (Thm. 4.4), and "hug the trim boundary" (values just inside
      the retained range).
    - Deliverable: a baseline x attack results table.

  Phase 4 - RL attacker (Formulation 5A)
    - Train with PPO or SAC against each baseline.
    - Exit: without being told to, the attacker finds the Ex. 7.2
      oscillation; it never violates Thm. 7.1 against LF; damage against [7]
      and [10] is reported. This also tests the [10] Prop. 1 concern
      empirically.

  Phase 5 - Shielded defender (Formulation 5B)
    - Write and check the shield theorem.
    - Implement a DeepSets or GNN policy and train it against the scripted
      attacks.
    - Exit: the proof is done; the defender beats plain LF error with zero
      safety violations.

  Phase 6 - Zero-sum training (Formulation 5C)
    - Train with alternating best responses or PSRO [20].
    - Exit: the defender's exploitability falls over training iterations and
      it holds up against held-out attackers.

  Phase 7 - Evaluation
    - Run the full grid in Section 7. Report the gap to the |T|/n bound,
      generalization to unseen graphs and F, and the ablations.

  Phase 8 - Paper
    - Write up and release the code. Venue options: IEEE TAC or L-CSS with
      CDC on the control side; AAMAS or NeurIPS on the learning side.


7. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every method is run against every attacker on the same graphs, functions and
seeds. Results are reported as mean +/- standard deviation over at least 10
seeds.

Graphs
  - Fig. 1 graph [1, Ex. 6.2], 5 nodes: not rooted after filtering; tests
    the (F+1, F+1) proof.
  - Complete graph [1, Ex. 7.2], 5 nodes: reproduces the oscillation attack.
  - U-W graph [1, Fig. 3, Ex. 8.2], 4K nodes (K >= 2): large maximum
    1-local set; error must reach b/4.
  - Random (2F+1)-robust graphs (constructions in [2], [16]), 20-100 nodes:
    main benchmark.
  - Erdos-Renyi above the threshold of [1, Sec. VIII], 20-100 nodes: small
    |T|/n; checks that the bound fades.

Functions
  Capped quadratics f_i(x) = (x - a_i)^2 with a_i drawn per episode, plus the
  two-cluster a/b assignment of [1, Prop. 8.1] for the lower-bound tests.
  Absolute-value functions are added as a non-smooth check.

Attackers
  Fixed value; random; switching (Ex. 7.2); stealthy fake cost (Thm. 4.4);
  trim-boundary hugging; learned RL attacker (Formulation 5A). Each is run in
  malicious and Byzantine variants, with F in {1, 2, 3}.

Methods compared
  #  Method                                Source                    Role
  1  Distributed subgradient descent       Nedic & Ozdaglar [3],[4]  No defence: shows the size of the threat
  2  LF dynamics                           Sundaram & Gharesifard [1] Main baseline; our method builds on it
  3  Byzantine-resilient optimization      Su & Vaidya [5]           Classical filtering baseline
  4  Q-consensus + gradient step           Hou et al. [7]            Prior RL defence (adapted)
  5  DRC-RSSG + gradient step              Zhang, Chen, Ji [10]      Newest game-based defence (adapted)
  6  Shielded RL defender                  This work                 Proposed (Formulation 5B)
  7  Shielded RL defender, game-trained    This work                 Proposed (Formulation 5C)

Metrics
  - Optimality error: |xbar_R(T) - x*| and f(xbar_R(T)) - f(x*), also as a
    fraction of the |T|/n * |b - a| bound.
  - Consensus: D(T) = M(T) - m(T), and the number of steps until
    D(t) < epsilon.
  - Safety: the share of runs in which any regular state leaves the hull of
    the regular minimizers. This must be 0 for methods 2, 6 and 7.
  - Oscillation: total variation of xbar_R(t) over the second half of the
    run.
  - Exploitability of the defenders: the damage done by a best-response RL
    attacker trained against that defender.

Ablations
  No shield (raw RL weights); shield without adversarial training;
  observation with or without history; DeepSets vs GNN policy; training F vs
  test F mismatch.


8. RISKS AND OPEN QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Risks (risk -> why it matters -> fallback)

  - The shield leaves too little freedom: learned weights barely beat uniform
    LF.
      Why: weakens C3 and C4.
      Fallback: let the policy also choose the trim level within [F, F_max],
      and prove a matching weaker guarantee; or report the honest null
      result, which still answers RQ3.

  - The |T|/n bound is loose, so the gap to it looks large even for a
    near-optimal defender.
      Why: makes C4 hard to read.
      Fallback: also compute the best achievable error for small graphs by
      exhaustive search over adversary placements.

  - Zero-sum training is unstable.
      Why: delays Phase 6.
      Fallback: use PSRO with a fixed population; fall back to a defender
      trained against the Phase 4 attacker only.

  - The [10] Prop. 1 concern turns out to be our misreading.
      Why: weakens G2.
      Fallback: keep G2 on [7] alone; frame [10] as complementary
      (no-regret, edge-based model).

  - A competing paper appears during the project.
      Why: novelty.
      Fallback: Phase 0 sweep, repeated before submission.

Open questions
  - Scalar only, as in [1], or a coordinate-wise multidimensional extension
    as a second paper? The plan assumes scalar.
  - Should the attacker also choose where the adversaries sit
    (topology-aware placement), or only what they send?
  - Timeline and target venue: to be agreed before Phase 1 starts.
